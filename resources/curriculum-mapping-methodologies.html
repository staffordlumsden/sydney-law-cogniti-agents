Curriculum Mapping Methodologies

Sydney Law School · Educational Design · June 2025

Updated for the current Cogniti resource hub: 3 September 2026

This resource sets out the qualitative methodology used to analyse how assessment tasks address Unit Learning Outcomes (ULOs), and how ULOs can be mapped to Course Learning Outcomes (CLOs), Sydney University Graduate Qualities and relevant external frameworks.

The methodology is intentionally human-reviewable. Numerical scores can make patterns visible, but they do not replace qualitative judgement about curriculum purpose, assessment design or the meaning of learning outcomes.

1. Analyse each assessment task

Review each assessment to identify:

  • Purpose: what knowledge, skills or attributes the task is intended to assess.
  • Nature of the task: for example, foundational knowledge, practical application, critical analysis, communication or ethical reasoning.
  • Expected outputs: what students must actually produce.
  • Assessment type: formative or summative; individual or collaborative.
  • Cognitive and professional demand: the level of reasoning, judgement or application required.

2. Map assessment tasks to ULOs

For each assessment task:

Identify relevant ULOs

Determine which ULOs correspond to the knowledge, skills or capabilities students must demonstrate.

Match task requirements to ULO verbs

Compare the actions required by the assessment with the verbs and demands expressed in each ULO, for example:

  • analyse;
  • evaluate;
  • communicate;
  • apply;
  • interpret; or
  • exercise judgement.

Evaluate task scope

Decide whether the assessment:

  • directly and comprehensively addresses the ULO;
  • addresses only part of the ULO; or
  • does not provide evidence of the ULO.

3. Score the alignment

The supplied methodology uses a simple 0–2 scale:

ScoreInterpretation
2The assessment directly and substantially addresses the ULO.
1The assessment partially or indirectly addresses the ULO.
0The assessment does not address the ULO.

4. Justify the alignment

For each assessment–ULO relationship, provide a short justification that explains:

  • how the task enables students to demonstrate the relevant knowledge, skill or attribute;
  • how the task structure supports that demonstration; and
  • whether the assessment suite provides sufficient coverage of the ULO overall.

The justification is important because a numeric score alone does not explain why an alignment judgement was made.

5. Consider assessment weighting

Where tasks have substantially different weightings, a weighted view can help show how strongly each ULO is represented in the summative assessment design.

A simple approach is:

  1. convert the assessment weighting to a proportion;
  2. multiply each alignment score by that proportion; and
  3. sum the weighted scores for each ULO.

For ULO 1, the illustrative weighted total is:

Weighting should be used cautiously. It adds another lens to the analysis; it does not turn curriculum alignment into a purely quantitative exercise.

6. Interpret the assessment–ULO matrix

Review the matrix for:

  • completeness: are all ULOs meaningfully assessed?
  • balance: are some ULOs over- or under-represented?
  • gaps: are any outcomes addressed only weakly or not at all?
  • proportionality: does the distribution of assessment effort reflect the importance of the outcomes?
  • fairness: does the assessment design give students reasonable opportunities to demonstrate each intended outcome?
  • coherence: do the assessment tasks collectively reflect the stated purpose and description of the unit?

7. Validate against learning frameworks

Where relevant, cross-check the mapping against:

  • the cognitive demand of the ULOs;
  • Australian Qualifications Framework expectations;
  • Council of Australian Law Deans Threshold Learning Outcomes;
  • Course Learning Outcomes; and
  • Sydney University Graduate Qualities.

The purpose is not to force every ULO to map to every framework. The aim is to identify defensible relationships and reveal omissions or inconsistencies.

8. Map ULOs to CLOs

ULO-to-CLO mapping asks whether the unit contributes meaningfully to broader course-level outcomes.

The analysis should consider:

  • the disciplinary knowledge or capability expressed in the ULO;
  • the scope and cognitive demand of the CLO;
  • whether the ULO contributes directly, partially or not at all;
  • whether the set of ULOs collectively supports the relevant CLOs; and
  • whether the relationship can be explained in a short qualitative justification.

This mapping is more discipline- and programme-specific than mapping to broad graduate qualities.

9. Map ULOs to Sydney University Graduate Qualities

ULO-to-Graduate-Quality mapping asks how a unit contributes to broader transferable capabilities expected of graduates.

The process uses the same broad logic as ULO-to-CLO mapping:

  1. analyse the ULO's verb, scope and intended capability;
  2. compare it with the relevant Graduate Quality;
  3. judge the strength of alignment;
  4. provide a qualitative justification; and
  5. review the overall pattern for balance and coverage.

The focus is broader than disciplinary CLO mapping and may include capabilities such as critical thinking, communication and cultural competence.

11. Iterative refinement

Curriculum mapping should be revisited as the analysis develops.

Refine the mapping to improve:

  • clarity of each relationship;
  • consistency of scoring;
  • balance across outcomes;
  • alignment with the unit's purpose;
  • coverage across the assessment suite; and
  • the explanatory justifications.

A useful workflow is to have the Cogniti agent produce an initial matrix and justification, then have the academic review, challenge and amend each judgement.

12. Using Unit Outline Helper

A concise prompt is:

Create a matrix showing the alignment between ULOs and assessments in this unit.

Use the following scale:
0 = not addressed
1 = partially addressed
2 = addressed

For every non-zero score, provide a concise justification based on what students are actually required to demonstrate in the assessment.
Then identify gaps, over-concentration and any ULOs that may require assessment redesign.

For broader mapping:

Analyse the Unit Learning Outcomes below against:
1. Course Learning Outcomes;
2. Sydney University Graduate Qualities;
3. relevant AQF expectations; and
4. relevant CALD Threshold Learning Outcomes.

Use a 0–2 alignment scale and provide a concise qualitative justification for each non-zero mapping.
Do not infer alignment from shared keywords alone; explain the substantive capability relationship.

13. Technology and implementation note

The earlier 2025 source included model-specific descriptions of GPT architecture, NLP, embeddings, fine-tuning and Python infrastructure. Those claims are not used as assumptions in this current resource, because the underlying implementation of a hosted Cogniti agent can change independently of the curriculum methodology.

For current practice, the important point is functional: the agent performs language-based comparison of curriculum text and generates proposed mappings and justifications. Staff should evaluate those outputs on their educational merits rather than treating any particular technical mechanism as established.

Optional computational alternative

If a curriculum team wants a separate reproducible text-similarity check, conventional Python tooling can be used as an independent analytical aid. For example:

from sklearn.feature_extraction.text import TfidfVectorizer
from sklearn.metrics.pairwise import cosine_similarity

ulos = [
    "Analyse and apply the principles of open justice and case management.",
    "Evaluate and implement appropriate Civil Dispute Resolution procedures."
]

clos = [
    "Demonstrate an integrated understanding of legal principles.",
    "Apply critical thinking to legal questions and problems."
]

vectorizer = TfidfVectorizer()
vectors = vectorizer.fit_transform(ulos + clos)

similarity_matrix = cosine_similarity(
    vectors[:len(ulos)],
    vectors[len(ulos):]
)

A similarity score is not itself evidence of curriculum alignment. Any computational comparison still requires qualitative interpretation by curriculum experts.

Final review principle

The final mapping should be transparent, defensible and human-reviewed. The agent can augment the analysis by structuring comparisons and surfacing possible relationships; academic and curriculum judgement determines whether those relationships are educationally valid.
